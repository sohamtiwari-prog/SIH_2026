The Moon does not change.
Only the light does.

Problem statement 26166 asks for a generic solution that finds correspondence between Chandrayaan-2 optical imagery and lunar reference imagery at sub-pixel accuracy, with match points uniformly distributed, delivered as software plus a registered product and evaluation metrics. This page reports what was built and what it measures, against a harness where the correct alignment is known exactly.

The statement names three obstacles — illumination, viewpoint and scale variation. Sections 01 to 06 take each in turn; 07 and 08 show why the design is what it is; 09 to 11 are the three required deliverables.

Sample sizes and method definitions are stated with each figure. Nothing on this page is estimated.

03 — challenge 1: why the failure peaks at 90°

180° is not the hardest case. 90° is.

Orientation is π-periodic. Rotating the sun by 180° maps every edge orientation back onto itself, so the dominant orientation channel is unchanged — only the contrast sign flips, which phase congruency already ignores. At 90° the channel genuinely rotates, and a different population of slopes is lit altogether.

Any design that assumes “larger azimuth difference means harder” will tune against the wrong failure mode.

06 — challenge 3: matching beyond the appearance limit

When appearance runs out, match constellations

Everything up to here matches appearance — intensity, phase congruency, gradient histograms. Appearance is a function of resolution, so at a 10:1 GSD ratio the coarse image simply does not contain the texture the fine image is made of. Measured: dense correlation lands 252–763 px out while still reporting convergence.

A 500 m crater, though, is 500 m wide in every image ever taken of it. Crater centres are geometric landmarks in metres, not appearance features in pixels, and their relative arrangement is a rigid property of the surface. So the question becomes “does this arrangement of craters match that arrangement” — the same problem a star tracker solves when it identifies its attitude from a pattern of stars without knowing what any individual star looks like.

Two things fall out for free: the method needs no GSD prior at all, which makes it the natural bootstrap when PDS labels are missing or wrong, and it is illumination-free, because a crater rim is circular whatever the sun does.

Detector or matcher? The harness can tell them apart

A crater pipeline that fails could be failing at detection or at matching, and from the outside these look identical. Because the synthetic harness knows where every crater actually is, the matcher can be run on the true catalogue — isolating one from the other.

The matcher is not the problem. On true crater positions it reaches 0.00–0.52 px at every ratio tested, including the 10:1 case where dense correlation is three orders of magnitude worse. The blocking component is detection on coarse imagery, where recall falls from 0.62–0.73 to 0.02–0.07 — one to six craters found out of roughly 120 present. That is a single, well-defined component to fix, and knowing which one is worth more than a marginal accuracy gain elsewhere.

Why the detector had to be rebuilt

The first detector used a Hough transform on the phase-congruency map and reached precision 0.25, recall 0.21. Hough looks for circular edges, but a lunar crater under oblique light is not an edge ring — it is a bowl whose shading ramps along the illumination direction. Solar azimuth is in every PDS label, so that ramp is known in advance and can be matched directly.

Replacing Hough with a matched filter against a bank of bowl templates roughly tripled recall. The sign of the template matters: the interior wall on the far side from the sun is the bright one, and inverting it produces a mound detector that correlates with terrain just well enough to look like it is working while precision sits near 0.01.

11 — evaluation metrics

Evaluation metrics

The statement asks for evaluation metrics, giving RMSE, inlier match count and inlier ratio as examples. All three are reported below for the product in section 10, together with the metrics needed to make them trustworthy.

Why RMSE and inlier ratio are not sufficient on their own

Every quality number reported so far measures self-consistency: whether the surviving matches agree with each other. They can agree perfectly and all be wrong together, which is exactly what the silent failures do.

So register the source onto the reference, then register the reference back onto the source, and compose the two. If both are right you end up where you started, and any deviation is error the pipeline produced without ever being told the answer. The two runs are genuinely different computations — different priors, different template windows, phase congruency computed on different data, different random draws — so their agreement is evidence in a way that one estimate agreeing with itself is not.

One caveat worth stating plainly: cycle error is a reliable gate, not a calibrated error bar. On the extreme-scale runs it reads 16 px where the truth is 119 px, so it tells you reliably that something is wrong without telling you how wrong. It costs one extra run of the pipeline, roughly doubling runtime.

12 — limitations

What this does not yet solve

    Reproducing these numbers

    A synthetic Moon is rendered from a crater-field DEM under two solar geometries and two sensor models, so the correct homography is written down rather than inferred. Every figure reads from the CSVs that harness emits.

    • python -m lunareg.experiments outputs — runs the four experiments, writes CSVs.
    • python -m lunareg.analyse — aggregates with bootstrap intervals, rebuilds this page's data.
    • Moving to real PDS products means replacing one function, the pair loader. The pipeline already reads GSD and solar azimuth from the label, which is what the scale prior and the orientation-shift prediction consume.
